GIL-DDI: multi-view graph invariant learning for unknown drug­drug interaction prediction
Yuanxian Li1,2 · Yuan Du1,3 · Hong Peng1 · Zhenli He1 · Xin Jin1 · Cheng Xie1
Received: 23 May 2025 / Revised: 16 September 2025 / Accepted: 17 October 2025 © The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer-Verlag London Ltd., part of Springer Nature 2025
Abstract Drug­drug interaction (DDI) prediction is essential for evaluating the side effects of a new drug and adverse interactions before the clinical application. The latest research applies multiview data to enhance the generalization ability of models to predict new drug interactions, mainly unknown drug­drug interaction (uDDI). However, a new drug's feature inevitably encounters the feature-shift problem; the trained models have not previously learned information about the new drug, significantly decreasing the uDDI prediction's accuracy. Thus, we proposed the GIL-DDI model that tries to extract the invariant features of known drugs, alleviating the impact of the feature-shift problem on the prediction of uDDI. In essence, a graph attention network (GAT) initially embeds multi-view knowledge graphs of known drugs, capturing features from chemical entities, substructures, and interactions. Inspired by invariant learning, we subsequently construct a robust feature space of these stable, known-drug characteristics. In the context of a novel pharmaceutical agent, the GAT is employed to initially embed its unique characteristics. Subsequently, these features are fused with the invariant features borrowed from the most similar known drugs in the feature space. This fusion enables the model to enhance the representation of the new drug, thereby addressing issues such as data scarcity and feature-shift problems. Extensive experiments on real-world drug datasets indicate that the proposed method achieves new state-of-the-art records on new drug DDI prediction tasks. The source code is available at https://github.com/davidwushi1145/GILDDI.

1 Introduction

With the growing prevalence of polypharmacy, the risk of adverse drug interactions is increasing, posing a significant threat to patient safety [1]. In this context, prediction of drug­drug interactions (DDI) provides a method to proactively identify potential interactions among drugs, thereby reducing toxic side effects and ensuring optimal therapeutic outcomes [2]. This proactive strategy enhances patient safety by minimizing the risks associated with medication errors and facilitates the development of personalized treatment regimens tailored to individual patient profiles [3].
In recent years, multi-view DDI analysis has gained significant attention due to its potential to provide a comprehensive understanding of drug­drug interactions. This approach leverages multiple perspectives, such as molecular structures, biological pathways, and pharmacological properties, to capture the complex relationships underlying DDIs. Researchers have made substantial progress in this field, which can be broadly categorized into two main directions: graph-based and feature-fused methods. The former focuses on improving prediction accuracy using designed graph-based methods. For example, the SumGNN [4] model introduces a subgraph extraction module for anchoring relevant subgraphs from noisy biomedical knowledge graphs, which significantly improves multiple types of DDI prediction accuracy, particularly in low-data scenarios. Additionally, SSI-DDI breaks down DDI prediction into substructure-level interactions, operating on raw molecular graphs to improve its interpretability and precision by modeling pairwise interactions between substructures [5]. The latter aims to exploit the intricate mechanisms underlying DDI events by integrating multiple drug-associated features, such as targets, enzymes, transporters, and molecular structures. DSIL-DDI [6] is a pluggable substructure interaction module that learns domaininvariant representations for DDI prediction, improving generalization and interpretability across transductive, inductive, and out-of-distribution settings. Recent efforts in this area include DSN-DDI [7], which employs a dual-view representation learning strategy, iteratively capturing local and global substructures to enhance prediction accuracy, especially for unseen drugs, while improving generalization to related tasks. Differing from this, MDFSA-DDI [8] integrates multi-source feature fusion with a transformer-based self-attention mechanism, enhancing prediction accuracy in DDI tasks.
However, predicting interactions for a new (unknown) drug poses a critical outof-distribution generalization challenge, which we term the feature-shift problem. This discrepancy is not merely a superficial difference in molecular structure; rather, it is a multifaceted issue rooted in the model's inability to generalize beyond its training data. Existing models are inadequate for several mechanistic reasons:
Chemical and structural shift The first issue to be addressed is that of a lack of chemical similarity anchoring [9]. New drugs may possess novel chemical scaffolds or functional groups that are absent from the training set. It has been demonstrated that models trained on known structures are incapable of generating reliable embeddings for these unseen chemical patterns.
Biological context shift Secondly, the latent biological context is absent [10]. The biological mechanisms of a new pharmaceutical agent, such as its primary and off-target interactions, metabolic pathways, or effects on transporters, may not be present in the training knowledge graphs. Consequently, the model is deficient in the requisite context to facilitate the inference of interactions.
Topological isolation in graphs Thirdly, the concept of "Topological Isolation in Graphs" is introduced [11, 12]. In graph-based models, a new drug is represented by an isolated or

As demonstrated in Fig. 1, the incorporation of a novel pharmaceutical agent into a model that has been trained exclusively on known drugs results in unreliable predictions. This is due to the fact that the model has not yet acquired the capacity to generalize across chemical, biological, and topological variations.
To address this challenge, we propose a novel unknown drug­drug interaction prediction method, named GIL-DDI. The core idea is that the training and predicting phases of the GIL-DDI model are separated to extract the invariant and variant features of unknown drugs for alleviating the feature-shift problem (Fig. 1). In detail, this approach uses graph attention network (GAT) models to embed multimodal drug-based knowledge graphs, incorporating drug­chemical entities, drug substructures, drug­drug interactions, and molecular structures, to obtain the representation of known drugs. Inspired by invariant learning, the invariant features for new drugs are derived by fusing these target drug-based graphs which are associated with new drugs. At the same time, the variant features specific to the unknown drug are also captured through the GAT model based on its unique features. Finally, the invariant and variant features of new drugs are both integrated to improve the prediction of DDI. By integrating these stable and consistent features, our approach mitigates the feature-shift problem and accurately captures specific characteristics of new drugs. This innovative methodology offers a promising solution to the limitations of existing models and advances the field of multi-view DDI analysis.

2 Related work
2.1 DDI analysis methods
2.1.1 Graph-based methods
The analysis of drug features is of paramount importance for the prediction of drug­drug interactions (DDI), although it presents significant challenges. Existing studies indicate that drugs with similar characteristics often exhibit comparable DDIs [13]. This observation has led to the development of methods based on measuring the similarity of drug features. DeepDDI [14] employs deep learning techniques to forecast interactions between drug pairs and between drugs and food constituents. KGNN [15] is an end-to-end framework for DDI prediction that captures drug and entity neighborhood information in knowledge graphs, modeling high-order structures and semantic relations to improve prediction accuracy. SafeDrug [16] is a DDI-controllable drug recommendation model that leverages drug molecule structures and explicitly models DDIs, achieving improved accuracy, reduced DDIs, and faster training and inference compared to previous methods. Recently, Wu et al. [17] proposed MKG-FENN, which employs multimodal knowledge graphs and end-to-end neural networks to predict DDI accurately.
2.1.2 Feature-fused methods
The feature-fused methods aim to exploit multiple drug-associated features for predicting DDIs. For example, Deng et al. [10] proposed a method based on drug feature similarity to predict DDIs. MDF-SA-DDI [8] presents an offline drug feature learning model that employs multi-source, multi-source feature fusion, and Transformer self-attention mechanisms. In contrast, ML-RDA [18] effectively leverages various drug features by incorporating an unsupervised disentangling loss, CuXCov. CASTER [19] predicts DDIs from chemical structures by using sequential pattern mining, auto-encoding with labeled and unlabeled data and dictionary learning for interpretable predictions, outperforming state-of-the-art methods on real-world datasets. DeSIDE-DDI [20] offers a deep learning-based framework for interpreting the underlying genetic mechanisms in DDI analysis. MDDI-SCL [21] represents a significant advancement in DDI prediction, employing supervised contrastive learning and three-level loss functions. ZeroDDI [22] proposes a dual-modal alignment strategy that uniformly distributes drug pair and DDI semantic representations in a unit sphere, aligning

The significant role of knowledge graphs (KGs) in synthesizing diverse information for pharmaceutical research is evident in applications such as mapping multimodal clinical data to KGs for disease diagnosis [23] and constructing multi-relational KGs for effective drug repurposing [24], thereby underscoring the value of multi-view perspectives for complex tasks like predicting unknown drug­drug interactions.

In contrast, the proposed GIL-DDI enhances the prediction of DDIs between novel drugs by integrating the variant characteristics of these drugs with the invariant features extracted from the multimodal knowledge graph.

2.2 The challenge of feature-shift in uDDI prediction
Within the domain of drug­drug interactions (DDIs), the feature-shift problem is characterized by a substantial decline in predictive efficacy when a model is exposed to medications not included in its training dataset. This is a paradigmatic out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization challenge, as previously defined in the literature [28]. In this scenario, the data distribution of the test set, which contains novel pharmaceutical agents, differs fundamentally from the training sett [29]. The problem can be deconstructed into three primary mechanistic failures for existing DDI models:
1. Chemical and structural shift DDI models are predicated on molecular structure for the purpose of predicting function. When a new pharmaceutical agent with an innovative chemical scaffold is introduced, its representation, for example, as a fingerprint or graph embedding, falls into a region of the feature space that was sparsely populated during the training process [9]. The model is devoid of "anchor points" based on chemical similarity that would reliably infer its interactive properties, resulting in unpredictable and often erroneous outputs.
2. Biological and mechanistic shift DDIs are fundamentally biological events that are mediated by proteins (targets, enzymes, transporters) and pathways. A newly developed pharmaceutical agent may interact with a novel set of proteins or influence pathways that are not associated with any drug in the training set. The absence of latent biological context signifies that even if the model has learned general rules about, for instance, CYP450 enzyme inhibition, it cannot apply them if the new drug's interaction with that enzyme family is unknown and unrepresented in the input data [10].
3. Topological shift in graph-based models The advent of graph neural networks (GNNs) in the domain of DDI prediction has given rise to a novel vulnerability. GNNs are designed to learn node representations by aggregating information from their neighbors in a graph

In order to overcome the aforementioned challenges, invariant learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm. By emphasizing invariant representations, models such as CIGA [31] and EERM [32] have demonstrated enhanced generalization on graph data. The present work is founded on the aforementioned principle, with the objective of designing a framework that explicitly learns invariant features from multi-view drug data. The aim is to bridge the gap between known and unknown drugs.
3 Methodology
In GIL-DDI, we propose a robust approach for drug­drug interaction (DDI) prediction using graph attention networks (GAT) to extract comprehensive drug features and a substructureupdatable codebook module to learn feature-invariant representations. Our method uniquely combines invariant features learned from existing drug knowledge with variant features specific to new drugs, enhancing prediction accuracy and generalization for new drugs and significantly improving the safety and efficacy of drug development. 3.1 Preliminaries

The manuscript focuses on four types of drug-based relationships essential for DDI events
and also refers to multi-view features. These features are obtained from four different views
and encoded into matrices: drug­drug, drug­entity, drug­substructure, and drug­molecular, which are denoted as Add, Ade, Ads, and Adm, respectively. All matrices were constructed
based on the initial embeddings derived from the respective knowledge graphs to capture the
fundamental characteristics of the drugs within the specific contextual framework.
Supposing we have dn drugs with ek entities, sk substructure, and mk molecular, we initialize different matrices according to their properties. Firstly, the initial step involves encoding DDIs into the drug­drug matrix Add  Rdn×dn . For example, if 65 distinct inter-
action events are represented in DDIs, we labeled them sequentially from 1 to 65, and the matrix Add  R65×65. Then, the rows and columns of the matrix correspond to individual drugs, with the value Aidjd indicating the interaction between the i-th and the j-th drugs. When no interaction is observed between two drugs, the corresponding entry is assigned a value of
0. Secondly, the drug­entity matrix Ade  Rdn×ek encodes drug-associated information,
including transporters, targets, and other relevant entities. In this matrix, the rows correspond
to the drugs, while the columns correspond to the associated entities. To define the relation-
ships between drugs and entities, we assign general functional roles to the entities represented by unique serial numbers. The value Aidje column denotes the serial number corresponding to the functional relationship between the drug i and the entity j.
Thirdly, the drug­substructure matrix Ads  Rdn×sk collates data on drug-associated
substructures. Similarly, its rows correspond to drugs, and columns represent the respective substructures. In particular, the matrix Ads is binary, where 1 indicates the presence of a
specific substructure in the corresponding drug, and 0 indicates its absence. Finally, the drug­molecular matrix Adm  Rdn×mk encodes information about the
molecular access system (MACCS), which comprises 13 MACCS bond descriptors and
7 supplementary molecular characteristics in this manuscript. Thus, the rows still represent
drugs, while the columns represent the MACCS bonds and molecular features.

3.3 Invariant-based graph learning

A significant challenge in DDI analysis pertains to the feature-shift problem, particularly the topological isolation of new drugs. To address this challenge, an invariant-based learning mechanism is proposed. The fundamental premise asserts that while a novel pharmaceutical agent's unique characteristics may be subject to variation, its essential properties, which are shared with established, well-understood medications, can be regarded as constant. The proposed methodology involves the enhancement of a drug's representation through the fusion of its representation with the averaged embedding of its most similar neighbors in the feature space. This process enables the model to "borrow" stable, context-rich features from known drugs, thereby enhancing robustness and improving DDI prediction, particularly in cases where data are scarce. 3.5 Algorithm design
By means of a detailed examination of the model, we proceeded to devise the Invariant Learning (IL) algorithm. The following section will present a description of the algorithmic flow.
The proposed Invariant-based drug­drug interaction (DDI) Prediction Algorithm employs a multi-view approach to enhance the accuracy of drug interaction predictions. The process commences with the construction of four invariant drug knowledge graphs, designated as A1, A2, A3, and A4, which are subsequently employed to initialize the corresponding drug matrices, A1, A2, A3, and A4. In the second step, the similarity between drug pairs is calculated using two distinct methods: the Jaccard similarity for views 1 and 2 and the Mahalanobis distance for views 3 and 4, based on the drug feature sets. In the third step, a self-attention mechanism is introduced. Here, the embeddings for each drug pair are initialized using attention scores computed from the drug feature vectors. 4.2 Experimental setups and tasks
Baselines The proposed GIL-DDI model is compared with seven state-of-the-art related models: MDF-SA-DDI [8], MKG-FENN [17], Lee methods [35], MDDI-SCL [21], MDNN [36], DDIMDL [10], and DeepDDI [14]. The traditional classification method, DNN, is also considered.
We evaluated the predictive performance of our model on datasets using multi-class classification analysis. All experiments were conducted using Python v3.8.19 and PyTorch v1.12.1, and results were computed on an NVIDIA RTX 2080Ti with 11 GB of memory. For Task 1, we use 10-fold cross-validation (10-CV) on drug­drug interactions (DDIs), partitioning all DDIs into ten subsets. Models are trained using DDI in the training set, and predictions are made for DDI in the test set. For Task 2 and Task 3, we apply 10-CV to the drugs instead of the DDI pairs. The drugs are divided into ten subsets, with nine subsets used as training drugs and the remaining subset used as test drugs. In Task 2, prediction models are developed using the DDI between two training drugs, and predictions are made for DDI events between a training drug and a test drug. In Task 3, prediction models are developed based on the DDI between two training drugs, and then predictions are made for DDI events between two test drugs.
In this study, three types of DDI prediction were evaluated in three experimental settings: (i) prediction of interactions between new drugs (Task 1); (ii) prediction of interactions between known drugs and new drugs (Task 2); and (iii) prediction of unobserved interactions between known drugs (Task 3). In these tasks, representations of new drugs are absent from the training set but present in the test set [37].

This strategy integrates a range of well-established techniques, including:
· Weight decay (L2 regularization) A weight decay of 1 × 10-8 was applied to the Adam optimizer. This penalty is imposed on large model weights, thereby discouraging the development of overly complex models and enhancing the generalization capabilities of the model.
· Dropout A dropout rate of 0.3 was applied within the graph attention network (GAT) layers. This technique involves the random deactivation of a subset of neurons during the training process. This approach is designed to prevent the model from becoming overly dependent on any specific feature or connection.
· Early stopping An early stopping criterion was implemented to monitor the model's performance on the validation set of each fold. The training process was terminated if the macro F1-score on the validation set did not demonstrate improvement for 100 consecutive epochs. The model parameters from the epoch with the best validation score were then restored for the final evaluation. This is a critical step in preventing the model from overfitting by halting the training at its point of optimal generalization.

4.8 Statistical analysis
To rigorously validate our findings and confirm that the observed performance improvements of GIL-DDI are not due to chance, we conducted a comprehensive statistical analysis. Following the recommended best practices for comparing multiple classifiers over multiple datasets (or, in this case, cross-validation folds), we adopted a nonparametric statistical framework [38]. This approach is preferable to parametric tests (e.g., the paired t test) as the performance scores across a small number of folds (k=10) often does not satisfy the normality assumption.
Our analysis proceeded in two stages. First, we employed the Friedman test as an omnibus test to determine if there were any statistically significant differences among the performance of all compared models. This test was conducted independently on the per-fold results for both the Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPR) and the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) from Task 3. The results for both metrics yielded extremely low p-values (AUPR:  2(9) = 434.55, p < 0.0001; AUC:  2(9) = 457.59, p < 0.0001), thereby enabling us to reject the null hypothesis that all models demonstrate equivalent performance and underscoring the necessity for post hoc analysis.
Secondly, after determining the overall significance, post hoc tests were conducted to identify specific pairwise differences. In order to directly test our primary hypothesis, we employed the one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test to compare GIL-DDI with each baseline method individually. As demonstrated in Table 8, GIL-DDI exhibited a statistically significant superiority over every baseline model on both AUPR and AUC metrics. 4.9 Qualitative analysis and biological validation
A significant challenge in predictive models for drug­drug interactions (DDI), particularly for graph-based neural networks, is ensuring model interpretability and validating the underlying mechanisms. To address this, and to substantiate the core claim of our paper--that GIL-DDI mitigates the feature-shift problem for unknown drugs by fusing learned invariant features-- we present a qualitative case study. This analysis aims to deconstruct the model's predictive process and connect its learned representations to tangible biological and chemical insights.

4.9.1 Defining invariant features in context
Firstly, it is imperative to establish a precise definition for the concept of "invariants features" as implemented in GIL-DDI. An invariant feature, in contrast to a globally fixed set of attributes, is a context-dependent representation that is dynamically generated for a specific unknown drug. The process entails the "borrowing" of stable, well-characterized properties from a cohort of drugs with the most similar properties in the learned multi-view feature space. This aggregated representation, which encapsulates the shared chemical motifs and biological functions of these neighbors, serves as the invariant feature. The integration of this with the intrinsic (or "variant") characteristics of the unknown drug serves to enhance the model's representation, thereby providing it with a robust biological context that would otherwise be absent.

4.9.2 Case study: predicting a uDDI for ritonavir
To illustrate the functionality of the mechanism, an experiment was conducted in which ritonavir was excluded from the training set. This approach emulates a genuine scenario of a drug­drug interaction (uDDI) prediction. The objective of this study was to predict its interaction with Lopinavir. Ground truth
In clinical practice, Ritonavir is a potent mechanism-based inhibitor of the Cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) enzyme. Lopinavir is a known substrate of CYP3A4. The concomitant administration of these medications results in a substantial increase in the DDI, as evidenced by the significant increase in the plasma concentration and therapeutic effect of Lopinavir that is observed when Ritonavir is administered concomitantly. An effective uDDI model must demonstrate the capability to predict this critical interaction without prior exposure to Ritonavir. GIL-DDI's predictive process
1. Initial variant embedding In the presence of Ritonavir, GIL-DDI initially produces an initial "variant" embedding based exclusively on its intrinsic properties, including its molecular graph and chemical descriptors from the "drug­molecular" perspective. At this stage, the representation is topologically isolated and lacks the relational context necessary for accurate DDI prediction.
2. Invariant feature extraction Subsequently, the model queries the feature space of known drugs to identify Ritonavir's nearest neighbors. The model identifies other potent CYP3A4 inhibitors, such as the antifungal drugs Ketoconazole and Itraconazole, as its closest analogs based on the presence of similarities in the drug­substructure and drug­entity views.

3. Biological and chemical insight The similarity identified by the model is not superficial but is rooted in fundamental biochemical principles.
· Chemical rationale A salient shared characteristic among these drugs is the presence of a nitrogen-containing heterocyclic ring, exemplified by a thiazole ring in ritonavir and imidazole/triazole rings in antifungals. This specific chemical moiety is mechanistically crucial, as its nitrogen atom directly coordinates with the heme iron in the active site of CYP450 enzymes, causing potent inhibition. This shared structural element constitutes the chemical basis of the invariant feature.
· Biological rationale By identifying Ketoconazole and Itraconazole as neighbors, GILDDI effectively recognizes that Ritonavir belongs to a class of drugs that strongly inhibit CYP3A4. The model proceeds to aggregate the embeddings of these neighbors to form the "invariant feature" for Ritonavir, which now encodes this critical biological property.
4. Feature fusion and prediction Finally, the initial variant embedding of Ritonavir is fused with this borrowed invariant feature. Consequently, the resulting enriched representation now comprehends Ritonavir not as an isolated entity, but as a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor. When tasked with predicting its interaction with Lopinavir (a known CYP3A4 substrate), the model correctly identifies the high-risk "inhibitor-substrate" pattern and predicts a significant DDI event.

This case study substantiates the hypothesis that GIL-DDI learns to associate specific, mechanistically relevant chemical substructures with their functional consequences in DDI events. The "invariant feature" for Ritonavir is a tangible representation of its CYP3A4 inhibitory function, which was learned by analogy from known drugs. This finding indicates that the model's approach offers a comprehensible solution to the feature-shift problem, thereby facilitating reliable predictions, even for novel pharmaceutical agents. To provide visual evidence for this claim, a t-SNE projection of the feature space was employed to illustrate the embedding of Ritonavir. It was observed that Ritonavir shifted from a sparse region to a densely populated cluster of known CYP3A4 inhibitors post-fusion, thereby confirming the successful transfer of this learned property (Fig. 10).

4.11.1 Complexity of GIL-DDI components
The computational cost of GIL-DDI can be analyzed in three main stages:
1. Graph construction and similarity calculation The initial construction of the four viewspecific matrices involves processing the raw data for all N drugs. The complexity of this step is proportional to the size of the input data, exhibiting an approximate linear relationship. This can be expressed as follows: O(N · Mavg), where Mavg denotes the
123

48 Page 28 of 37

Y. Li et al.

average number of features across views. The subsequent similarity calculation (e.g., Jaccard or Mahalanobis distance) between all pairs of drugs to form similarity matrices Sv has a complexity of O(N 2 · Mavg). 2. Multi-view graph attention network (GAT) The core of our feature extraction process is the GAT module. In contrast to GNNs, which operate on a pre-defined sparse graph structure, our model computes attention coefficients between all pairs of drugs to capture a global relationship context within each view. The calculation of attention scores for all drugs against all others results in a time complexity of O(N 2 · d) per GAT layer. This particular aspect of the model, namely the quadratic scaling with the number of drugs, is the most computationally intensive. 3. Invariant-based feature fusion This iterative process enhances node embeddings by aggregating features from the top-K most similar neighbors. Assuming that the top-K neighbors have been identified from the pre-computed similarity matrix, each of the L fusion steps involves the aggregation of features for N drugs from their respective K neighbors. This results in a complexity of O(L · N · K · d). Given that K is a small hyperparameter (e.g., K = 6), this stage is significantly more efficient than the GAT layer.
The predominant factor contributing to GIL-DDI's complexity is the GAT's pairwise attention mechanism, which renders the model's runtime scale quadratically with the number of drugs, i.e., O(N 2 · d).

4.11.2 Architectural comparison and scalability
The efficacy of GIL-DDI can be contextualized through a comparative analysis of its architectural design with that of the baselines:
· DNN-based models (e.g., DeepDDI) These models generally process a fixed-size feature vector for each drug pair through a series of fully connected layers. The complexity of these networks is primarily determined by the network's depth and width, rather than by the number of drugs, N . While they are computationally efficient per-pair, they cannot directly model high-order relationships across the entire drug graph without explicit feature engineering.
· Standard GNN-based models (e.g., MKG-FENN) A significant number of GNNs function within the context of sparse biological knowledge graphs, characterized by a substantially smaller number of edges, |E|, relative to the total number of edges, N 2. The complexity of a message-passing layer in such models is typically O(|E|·d), which is highly scalable for large, sparse graphs. The model's utilization of a dense, dynamically computed attention graph constitutes a deliberate trade-off, prioritizing expressive power to capture global inter-drug relationships over the scalability of sparse GNNs.
· Transformer-based models (e.g., MDF-SA-DDI) These models utilize a self-attention mechanism that, in a manner analogous to our GAT, computes pairwise interactions between all input elements. The complexity of a standard transformer encoder is O(S2·d), where S is the sequence length. Within the context of drug administration, the treatment of all drugs as a set of tokens results in an O(N 2 · d) complexity. Consequently, GIL-DDI shares its primary computational bottleneck with Transformer-based architectures. These architectures are both powerful but face scalability challenges with a very large number of drugs.
In summary, the design of GIL-DDI prioritizes the rich, global modeling of drug­drug relationships through a dense attention mechanism. While this results in a quadratic time

complexity with respect to the number of drugs, it is a well-reasoned trade-off for enhancing predictive accuracy on datasets of moderate size, such as those commonly used in DDI research. In the context of future endeavors involving datasets comprising tens of thousands of drugs, scalability enhancements can be realized through the incorporation of sampling techniques or more advanced sparse attention mechanisms.

5 Conclusion
5.1 Summary
This study proposes a novel GIL-DDI model for predicting drug­drug interactions (DDI). The GIL-DDI model addresses the feature-shift problem by integrating invariant features of known drugs with variant features specific to new drugs. The model employs graph neural networks (GNNs) to embed multiview knowledge graphs into comprehensive drug features, including drug­chemical entities, drug substructures, drug­drug interactions, and molecular structures.
The model employs a self-attention mechanism to fuse these features, enhancing its generalization capability and improving prediction accuracy. Extensive experiments on real-world drug datasets demonstrate that the GIL-DDI model achieves state-of-the-art performance, consistently outperforming existing approaches across various metrics.
In conclusion, the GIL-DDI model provides a robust solution for accurately predicting drug interactions, thereby contributing to enhanced patient safety and the development of more personalized treatment regimens.
5.2 Limitations and future work
Notwithstanding the substantial advancements achieved by the proposed GIL-DDI model in addressing the feature-shift problem in novel drug­drug interaction (DDI) prediction, several limitations persist. These limitations also reveal promising avenues for future research.
5.2.1 Current work limitations
Simplification of static and binary interactions In accordance with the majority of contemporary computational models, GIL-DDI conceptualizes DDIs as static, context-independent occurrences, as previously outlined by Wang et al. [39]. However, in clinical practice, the actual effects of drug combinations are highly dependent on various factors, including dosage, administration regimen, patient genetic background, and co-existing disease states. Additionally, the primary focus of our model is the prediction of binary variables. DDIs do not model higher-order interactions involving three or more drugs, which are increasingly common in polypharmacy patients [40].
Task formulation and dataset specificity The present study has been meticulously engineered to address the multi-class classification of pharmacological DDI mechanisms, a task for which curated knowledge bases such as DrugBank are regarded as the gold standard. Consequently, the generalizability of our model has been validated on datasets aligned with this task. However, the performance of the model on datasets structured for different tasks, such as the multi-label prediction of clinical polypharmacy side effects (e.g., TWOSIDES, Decagon), remains to be evaluated. A direct application of these datasets would constitute
123

48 Page 30 of 37

Y. Li et al.

a task-transfer challenge, requiring significant reformulation of the model architecture and prediction objective.
Inherent data challenges The model's reliance on public databases is predicated on the recognition that these databases are inherently limited in scope and depth. A class imbalance problem is first noted, with a paucity of known interacting drug pairs relative to unreported ones. This imbalance potentially causes the model to bias predictions toward the majority class, i.e., "no interaction" [41]. Secondly, the absence of an empirically validated "gold standard" negative DDI dataset compels the assumption that unlabeled drug pairs are negative, which may introduce label noise.
Model interpretability Despite the demonstrated efficacy of GIL-DDI in terms of predictive accuracy, the underlying graph neural networks (GNNs) persist in their "black box" configuration, thereby impeding comprehensive understanding and analysis. While the model can predict potential interactions between two drugs, it cannot explicitly elucidate the key molecular substructures or biological pathways that inform its predictions. This deficiency in interpretability represents a significant impediment to the comprehensive reliance and implementation of sophisticated computational models within clinical practice [41].
5.2.2 Future research directions
The aforementioned limitations indicate clear and impactful directions for future research, aiming to advance DDI prediction from general models toward truly personalized precision medicine.
Developing context-aware and personalized DDI prediction frameworks A fundamental future direction entails the development of multimodal fusion models with the capacity to integrate diverse, heterogeneous data sources, as outlined in the study by Tong et al. [42]. The following methods have been demonstrated to achieve this objective:
· Integrating clinical context The construction of patient-specific profiles from longitudinal Electronic Health Records (EHRs) has been demonstrated to facilitate the prediction of DDI risks within the context of a patient's comprehensive medical history and medication regimen, as evidenced by the work of Chen et al. [43].
· Incorporating genomic information The integration of patient pharmacogenomic (PGx) data has emerged as a critical approach in the prediction of complex drug­drug­gene interactions (DDGIs). This approach will facilitate the accounting for variations in DDI risk attributable to individual genetic polymorphisms in drug-metabolizing enzymes (e.g., CYP450) [44].
· Advanced fusion architectures The integration of molecular structure data of drugs, electronic health record (EHR) data of patients, and genomic variation data into a unified prediction model through the utilization of heterogeneous graph neural networks (HGNNs) or attention-based fusion models represents a pivotal technical approach to achieving this objective [45].
Extending to polypharmacy side-effect prediction A promising direction for future research is to adapt the core principles of GIL-DDI to the multi-label task of predicting polypharmacy side effects. This would entail the modification of the model to accommodate the distinct structure of datasets such as TWOSIDES and Decagon. The invariant learning mechanism, which involves the utilization of features from known entities to characterize new ones, has the potential to be highly effective in predicting the side-effect profiles of new drugs when employed in conjunction with existing medications.
Enhancing methodological robustness and interpretability

· Addressing data imbalance In future studies, researchers should consider adopting more advanced strategies to address the class imbalance problem. These strategies may include the implementation of Focal Loss functions or the development of Graph Contrastive Learning frameworks. The objective of these frameworks would be to learn drug representations that are more robust to class imbalance [46].
· Improving model interpretability The integration of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques, such as GNNExplainer, to identify key molecular fragments or biological targets that contribute most to a drug­drug interaction (DDI) prediction will be a crucial step in enhancing model transparency and clinical trustworthiness [47].
Enriching drug representations for higher fidelity The prevailing models in the field primarily rely on two-dimensional (2D) molecular structures. In the future, research endeavors may yield more sophisticated and context-aware molecular embeddings. These advancements may be achieved through two approaches. Firstly, the integration of three-dimensional (3D) conformational information could be employed to enhance molecular embeddings. Secondly, large-scale biochemical language models (e.g., MoLlama) could be utilized for pre-training, thereby ensuring the capture of geometric and electronic properties that are crucial for interactions. This approach is outlined in detail in Liu et al. [48].
By addressing these challenges, it is hypothesized that the field of computational DDI prediction will be able to provide more precise, personalized, and trustworthy support for clinical decision-making, ultimately enhancing patient medication safety.
